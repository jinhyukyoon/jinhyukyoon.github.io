Cable-driven Haptic Interface

Maximum workspace and comparative study

01

Haptic interface design for maximum workspace and force exertion

My role: project lead, system design/control/analysis, user experiment

Collaborator: Donghyeon Lee, Junyong Bang, Hyung Gon Shin

Advisor: Wan Kyun Chung, Seungmoon Choi, Keehoon Kim

Jinhyuk Yoon, Donghyeon Lee, Junyong Bang, Hyung Gon Shin, Wan Kyun Chung, Seungmoon Choi, and Keehoon Kim. Cable-Driven Haptic Interface with Movable Bases Achieving Maximum Workspace and Isotropic Force Exertion. IEEE Transactions on Haptics, vol. 16, no. 3, pp. 365-378, 2023.

Haptic feedback can make learning more effective, but virtual educational content still offers little of it.

This work proposes a planar cable-driven haptic interface with movable bases. It displays isotropic force feedback over a workspace extended to cover a commercial screen display. We derive a generalized kinematic and static analysis of the cable-driven mechanism that accounts for movable pulleys. Based on this analysis, we designed and controlled the system, including the movable bases, to maximize the workspace with isotropic force exertion over the target screen area. We evaluated the system as a haptic interface by workspace, isotropic force-feedback range, bandwidth, Z-width, and a user experiment. The workspace covered the target rectangular area, and the isotropic force reached up to 94.0% of the theoretical value within it.

02

Comparative user study

My role: system design & control

Collaborator: Dajin Lee, Daehyeon Nam, Dukchan Yoon

Advisor: Seungmoon Choi, Keehoon Kim

Dajin Lee, Daehyeon Nam, Jinhyuk Yoon, Dukchan Yoon, Seokwon Jeong, Keehoon Kim, and Seungmoon Choi. A Comparative Study of Physical and Haptic Exhibits in an Informal Learning Environment IEEE Transactions on Haptics, vol. 17, no. 4, pp. 557-566, 2024.

Virtual exhibits with haptic feedback make content easier to diversify and add digital affordance, at a lower cost than physical exhibits. Few studies, however, have examined their value in informal learning environments.

We tested whether a haptic exhibit could replace or supplement a traditional physical exhibit in a science museum. We built a two-degree-of-freedom cable-driven haptic device that simulates physical interaction on a large visual display. Based on a seesaw-like exhibit in a local museum, we implemented a virtual lever simulation that embodies the same physics principles. We then ran an observational study with children, comparing interaction behavior, learning effects, and self-reported motivation and enjoyment between the physical and virtual exhibits. Visitors received the haptic exhibit well and engaged with it, showing its potential for diverse learning settings.
